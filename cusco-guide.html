You land at 1 AM at 11,150 feet

Cusco sits at 3,399 m. That is higher than any resort town in Colorado and roughly 760 m above Bogotá, where you last acclimatized. A 1 AM arrival is the hardest possible version of this because you go from a pressurized cabin to sleeping altitude with no daylight, no food, and no time to adjust. Here is the protocol.

!

The single rule for the first 48 hours

Do not run, do not hike, do not drink alcohol, and do not carry your own bags up a flight of stairs. Altitude sickness in Cusco is not a rumour. It sends people to a clinic every single day, and the ones it hits hardest are fit endurance athletes who feel fine on arrival and decide to test it. Your fitness is the risk factor here, not the protection.

Landing night and the three days after

1 AM · touchdown Move slowly through the terminal. Prearrange the hotel transfer so you are not negotiating a taxi at 1 AM. Drink 500 ml of water in the car. Do not eat a heavy meal. At the hotel ask for coca tea (mate de coca) and for the oxygen concentrator location. Almost every decent Cusco hotel keeps one at reception.
Night one · sleep Sleep propped on two pillows rather than flat. Breathing gets shallow at altitude during sleep and the first night is usually broken. That is normal. Waking at 4 AM with a dry mouth and a light headache is normal. Waking unable to catch your breath, or with a headache that ibuprofen will not touch, is not. Keep water on the nightstand and finish it.
Day one · flat only Zero training. Walk the Plaza de Armas slowly, sit in the sun, eat light and often. Target 3 to 4 litres of water. Soup, quinoa, potatoes, and simple carbohydrate absorb well. Fat and red meat do not. Skip the pisco sour on night one no matter how good it looks. Altitude roughly doubles the effect of alcohol.
Day two · still nothing Still no running. A long gentle walk through San Blas is the correct dose. The uphill cobbles will make you stop and breathe, and that is exactly the information you want before you try any real effort. If you feel completely clean by evening, you are on schedule.
Day three · first run Twenty to thirty minutes, flat, conversational, heart rate capped well below your easy ceiling. Expect your heart rate to sit 10 to 20 beats higher than normal for the same effort and your pace to be 45 to 90 seconds per mile slower. That gap is the altitude, not lost fitness, and it narrows over the following week.
Day five onward Now you can train properly. This is where Cusco starts paying you back for the miserable first two days. See the Sacred Valley weekend below for how to turn the rest of the stay into a genuine altitude block for the Julian Alps 50K.

Sleeping altitude

3,399 m

11,150 ft. High enough that the acclimatization response is real and useful, and high enough that the first two days genuinely hurt. Oxygen availability is roughly 66 percent of sea level.

Coca leaf

Mate

Coca tea and coca leaf chewing are legal, everywhere, and free in most hotel lobbies. It helps with the headache and the lethargy. It will not prevent altitude sickness, and it can show on a drug test for weeks. Worth knowing if you get tested for anything.

Sorojchi pills

S/ 25

Sold over the counter at any InkaFarma or MiFarma. Mostly aspirin, salicylamide, and caffeine. They dull the symptoms rather than treat the cause. Acetazolamide (Diamox) is the real preventive and is also available over the counter here, but that is a conversation for your doctor before you fly.

If it gets bad

Descend

Clínica Pardo and Clínica San Juan de Dios both handle altitude cases around the clock and both have oxygen. The definitive treatment is always altitude loss. The Sacred Valley at 2,800 m is a 60 minute drive and that 600 m drop is usually enough to reset you completely.

Red flags that mean go to a clinic now, not in the morning

  • A headache that does not respond to ibuprofen after two doses.
  • Vomiting, or being unable to keep water down.
  • Breathlessness at rest, sitting still, not exerting.
  • A wet or crackling cough, or pink froth. This is fluid in the lungs and it is an emergency.
  • Confusion, slurred speech, or an inability to walk a straight line. This is fluid on the brain and it is a bigger emergency.
  • Any of the above means oxygen and descent immediately. Do not sleep on it and see how you feel.
01 · Orientation

Currency, safety, and getting around

Cusco runs on soles, on cash more than you expect, and on a tourist economy that has been doing this for fifty years. It is a safe city with a specific and predictable petty theft problem. Handle that one thing well and the rest is easy.

Currency

PEN

The Peruvian sol, written S/. Roughly 3.7 to the US dollar, so a quick mental shortcut is to divide by four and know you are being slightly conservative. Dollars are accepted at hotels and tour agencies at a poor rate. Pay in soles everywhere else.

Cash and cards

Both

Restaurants, hotels, and shops in the centre take cards. Markets, taxis, small cafés, and the artisan stalls do not. Keep S/ 200 to 300 in small notes. Nobody in a market can break a S/ 100 note and they will send you away rather than try.

ATMs

BCP

Use BCP, Interbank, BBVA, or Scotiabank, and use machines inside a bank branch or inside Real Plaza rather than a street kiosk. Decline the machine's own currency conversion every time. The casas de cambio along Avenida El Sol give better rates than any bank for dollars.

August weather

2 to 20 °C

Peak dry season, which is the best weather Cusco gets. Brilliant sun, deep blue sky, almost no rain. The swing is the story: near freezing at dawn, twenty degrees by early afternoon, cold again the moment the sun drops. Layers, always.

UV

Extreme

At 3,400 m in the tropics the UV index regularly hits 14 and above. This is genuinely the highest UV exposure you will encounter anywhere. Sunscreen every morning, a hat on every run, and lip balm with SPF. People burn badly here on cold days because it never feels hot.

Getting around

InDriver

InDriver is the dominant app in Cusco and Cabify also works. Uber coverage is thinner here than in Lima. The historic centre is walkable end to end in twenty minutes, though the cobbles and the gradient make that twenty minutes feel longer than it should. Never take an unmarked street taxi at night.

Water

Bottled

Do not drink the tap water. Given you need 3 to 4 litres a day at this altitude, buy the large bottles from a supermarket rather than small ones from a shop. Orion and Plaza Vea both sell 7 litre jugs cheaply.

Peruvian coffee

Origin

Peru is a serious producing country and the Cusco region grows its own in the Quillabamba and La Convención valleys, a few hours north and considerably lower. Ask for Peruvian single origin and you will drink very well for very little.

Safety, honestly

  • Violent crime against tourists is rare. Cusco is safer than most large Latin American cities and far safer than Lima.
  • Pickpocketing is the actual risk and it is concentrated in three places: the Plaza de Armas at night, the San Pedro market, and any crowded bus or festival.
  • Phone snatching is common. Do not walk down a busy street holding your phone out in front of you, and do not leave it on a restaurant table.
  • The scam pattern is distraction. Someone spills something on you, someone drops something in front of you, someone in a fake uniform asks for your documents. Keep walking.
  • Only take taxis you booked through InDriver or Cabify, or that your hotel called. Express kidnapping in unmarked taxis is a real Peru problem, though far more of a Lima one.
  • San Blas and the centre are fine to walk in the evening. The area around the Wanchaq bus terminal and the outer edges of San Pedro are not, after dark.
  • Emergency numbers: 105 for police, 116 for fire and ambulance. Tourist police (POLTUR) have an office on Avenida El Sol.
  • Photograph your passport and keep a copy in your email. Carry the copy, leave the original in the hotel safe.
02 · The Table

Where to eat across twelve days

Peru is the best eating country in South America and Cusco punches well above a city of half a million because the tourism economy funded a real restaurant scene. Book the top tier a few days ahead. Everything below is walkable from the centre or a S/ 10 ride.

Book these before you arrive
01
MAP Café
A glass and steel cube dropped into the courtyard of the Museo de Arte Precolombino, a colonial mansion off Plaza Nazarenas. Contemporary Andean tasting menus, immaculate service, and the most beautiful dining room in the city by a wide margin. The obvious choice for one special night.
Plaza Nazarenas
02
Chicha por Gastón Acurio
Gastón Acurio is the chef who made Peruvian food a global category, and Chicha is his Cusco room. Regional Andean cooking done with Lima technique. A second floor balcony over Plaza Regocijo. Consistently excellent and easier to book than MAP.
Plaza Regocijo
03
Cicciolina
The institution. Upstairs on Calle Triunfo, a warm chaotic room with a long tapas bar and Mediterranean cooking that has been the local benchmark for two decades. Sit at the bar without a reservation and order across the small plates. The one place everyone in Cusco agrees on.
Triunfo 393
04
Senzo at Palacio Nazarenas
The Belmond property's restaurant and the most polished fine dining in Cusco. Andean ingredients through a refined lens, a proper wine list, and a courtyard that used to be a convent. Expensive by Peru standards and still a fraction of the equivalent anywhere else.
Plaza Nazarenas
The strong everyday list

Limo
Nikkei cooking, the Peruvian Japanese hybrid, on a balcony over the Plaza de Armas. The ceviche and tiradito are the reason to come and the pisco list is long. Ask for a window table when you book.
Plaza de Armas

Uchu Peruvian Steakhouse
Alpaca, beef, and fish brought to the table raw on a volcanic stone heated to 400 degrees so you cook it yourself. Gimmicky in description, genuinely excellent in practice. Alpaca is lean, iron rich, and the best red meat choice at altitude.
Palacio 135

Marcelo Batata
A rooftop terrace with a view over the tiled roofs, modern Andean cooking, and the best chicha morada in town. They also run a well regarded cooking class if you want an afternoon of it. Reliable, warm, and good for a long lunch.

A big open courtyard in San Blas with a wood fired oven and blankets on the chairs for when the sun goes. This is where you eat cuy if you are going to eat cuy, and the oven roasted lamb and stuffed rocoto are outstanding regardless. Order the anticuchos.

Triunfo 370

Farm to table in the literal sense. They grow it on their own plot in the Sacred Valley and drive it up. The cleanest eating in Cusco and the best option on a day when you have trained and want vegetables that taste like something.
San Blas

Modern Peruvian in a bright informal room a block off the plaza. Good ceviche, good causa, good quinoa dishes, fast service. The dependable weekday lunch.

03 · The Cup

Coffee in a producing country

Peru is a top ten global coffee exporter and most of what leaves the country goes out as anonymous commodity beans. The specialty scene in Cusco exists to reverse that. Ask for Peruvian single origin, ask where it came from, and expect a real answer.

A flat white in Cusco costs about S/ 12, roughly three dollars, and the quality is comparable to anything in Melbourne or Portland. Most of these open at 7 AM or earlier, which suits a runner's schedule.
Best in city

Three Monkeys Coffee

Choquechaca 366, San Blas

The reference point for specialty coffee in Cusco. Direct relationships with Peruvian producers, careful roasting, baristas who can actually talk you through what you are drinking. A sunny courtyard, good breakfast, strong wifi. This is your default morning.

Origin story

Museo del Café

Espaderos 136

Part café, part working education on Peruvian coffee, in a colonial courtyard a block from the plaza. Tastings across regions, a proper roastery presence, and beans to take home. The place to go if you want to understand what Quillabamba coffee actually is.

Design

L'Atelier Café Concept

Cuesta San Blas

A café and antique shop in the same space, halfway up the San Blas hill. Everything you sit on is for sale. Excellent espresso, French leaning pastries, and a terrace with one of the better rooftop views in the barrio. Worth the climb, once you are acclimatized.

Roaster

Qura Café

San Blas

Small, serious, and roasting on site. Pour over focused, rotating single origins from Cusco region farms, and a barista who will happily spend ten minutes on the subject. Fewer seats than Three Monkeys and a quieter room.

Institution

Café Ayllu

Portal de Carnes 208, Plaza de Armas

Open since 1959 and still full of Cusqueños rather than tourists, which in a plaza location is an achievement. Classic espresso, hot chocolate, and a leche asada that people come specifically for. Not specialty coffee. Very much worth going.

Bakery

La Valeriana

Multiple locations, centre

The pastry answer rather than the coffee answer. Cusco's best bakery chain, reliably good empanadas and cakes, and open early. The one you stop at on the way somewhere else.

Social enterprise

The Meeting Place

Plazoleta San Blas

A café on the San Blas square that puts its profits into local community projects. Big breakfasts, bottomless filter coffee, and a noticeboard that is the best place in the city to find out what is actually happening this week.

Work session

Yaku Coffee

Centre

Calm, well lit, good wifi, and tolerant of a long sit. When you need two hours on a laptop and a flat white that will not disappoint you, this is the room.

04 · The Weekend

Sacred Valley: your Julian Alps 50K altitude block

This is the reason to be here. You are living at 3,399 m and the Sacred Valley floor sits around 2,800 m, an hour away. Sleep high, train slightly lower, and you have the classic altitude camp structure without engineering anything. Go on the second weekend, once you have five or six days of acclimatization behind you.

Why this works for a 50K in Slovenia

The Julian Alps 50K is a long day with sustained climbing at moderate altitude, and the limiting factor is aerobic ceiling under fatigue rather than raw speed. Two weeks living above 3,300 m drives the adaptation you want. The Sacred Valley then gives you what Cusco cannot: a valley floor flat enough for a genuine long run, plus climbs at Moray and Chinchero that replicate Alpine gradient at an altitude that makes them harder than the race will be. Train at 2,800 to 3,700 m in August, race at 1,500 to 2,300 m in the autumn, and the race feels like breathing at sea level.

The shape of the weekend

Friday afternoon Drive down to Urubamba, about 75 minutes. The road climbs to 3,700 m over the pass at Chinchero and then drops the whole way into the valley. Check in and notice how much easier breathing gets at 2,871 m. Eat early at the hotel. Sleep is markedly better down here and that alone is worth the trip.
Saturday · the long run Urubamba to Ollantaytambo along the valley floor, following the Río Urubamba. About 20 km one way at a near constant 2,800 m, on a mix of quiet road and dirt track, with the Andes on both sides the entire way. Start at 6:30 AM when the valley is still and cold. Have the driver meet you in Ollantaytambo with fluids, or turn at 10 km for an out and back. This is one of the great long run settings anywhere in the world and it will not feel like work.
Saturday afternoon Recover properly. Ollantaytambo is the best preserved Inca town in Peru and the terraces above it are worth an hour of slow walking, not more. Lunch in the square. Back to Urubamba, legs up, big carbohydrate dinner. Tambo del Inka and Sol y Luna both have proper pools if you want to float.
Sunday · the vertical day Drive up to Moray at 3,500 m and run the dirt roads through the concentric Inca agricultural terraces, then push on toward the Maras salt pans. Rolling to steep, high, exposed, and almost empty at that hour. Ninety minutes with real climbing in it is the correct dose. This is your Julian Alps simulation: gradient plus altitude plus dry air.
Sunday afternoon Stop at Maras salt pans on the way out, thousands of white terraced pools on a hillside that have been worked since before the Inca. Then Chinchero for the weaving cooperatives, at 3,762 m and higher than Cusco, so keep it to a walk. Back in Cusco by evening.
Best base

Tambo del Inka, Urubamba

Luxury Collection · 2,871 m

A Marriott Luxury Collection property on the river with the best pool in the valley and its own private rail platform for the Machu Picchu train. The most comfortable recovery base and the easiest logistics if you add Machu Picchu on.

The heated indoor and outdoor pool is genuinely useful after a 20 km valley run.

Most beautiful

Sol y Luna

Relais & Châteaux · Urubamba

Individual casitas in gardens under the mountains, a working equestrian centre, and a foundation school it funds. The most charming place to stay in the valley and the food is excellent. Book well ahead in August.

Design led

Explora Valle Sagrado

Urubamba · former hacienda

Built around daily guided exploration, with a large stable of hiking and biking routes leaving from the property. If you would rather have someone else design the vertical day, this is the one. All inclusive structure.

Logistics

Getting there and back

Cusco to Urubamba · 75 min

Hire a private driver for the whole weekend, roughly S/ 400 to 600, and you get support on the long run as part of the deal. Far better value than three separate transfers and it means someone is carrying your fluids on Saturday.

Agree the Saturday meeting point in Ollantaytambo before you start running. Mobile signal is patchy along the valley floor.

If you add it

Machu Picchu

Train from Ollantaytambo

Tickets are timed, capped, and sell out weeks ahead in August. Buy before you fly. The train leaves from Ollantaytambo, which is exactly where Saturday's long run finishes, so the sequencing is natural. Machu Picchu sits at 2,430 m, lower than Cusco, so it is an easy day physically.

Huayna Picchu and Huchuy Picchu are separate ticketed add ons with their own quotas. Decide when you book.

In city training

Sacsayhuamán loop, Cusco

Above San Blas · 3,600 to 3,800 m

Your midweek run. Climb out of San Blas to the Sacsayhuamán ruins, then follow the rolling dirt out past Q'enqo, Puka Pukara, and Tambomachay. Grass, dirt, and eucalyptus at 3,700 m with the city below you. Fifteen to twenty km out and back is available. Go at first light and take the site entry ticket if you plan to run through the ruins themselves.

This is the hardest running you will do all trip. Save it for day seven or later.

05 · The Work

Gyms, pilates, and studios

Cusco is a small city and the boutique fitness scene reflects that. There is one proper full service gym, a handful of good yoga studios, and a pilates and pole scene that runs largely through Instagram rather than websites. Here is the honest picture.

Gyms
Best equipped

Bodytech Cusco

Real Plaza, Av. Collasuyo

Peru's serious gym chain, inside the Real Plaza mall. Full free weight floor, machines, cardio, and group classes. Day passes and weekly passes are both available at the desk. Ask for a pase diario. The only place in Cusco with a genuinely complete strength setup.

Bring your passport. They usually want ID to issue a visitor pass.

Convenient

Hotel gyms in the centre

Various · centre

Several centre hotels sell gym access to non guests, and the JW Marriott and Palacio del Inka both have decent small gyms. Worth asking if you want ten minutes of walking rather than a ride out to Collasuyo on a recovery day.

Track

Estadio Garcilaso

Wanchaq · 3,350 m

The city stadium has a track that is sometimes open to the public in the mornings. If you want a controlled surface for strides or a tempo effort without cobbles or traffic, ask at the gate. Access is inconsistent, so treat it as a bonus rather than a plan.

Flat running

Avenida de la Cultura corridor

Wanchaq · flattest ground in Cusco

The historic centre is all gradient and cobbles, which is fine for hiking and hostile for easy running. The Wanchaq side of the city along Avenida de la Cultura is the flattest continuous ground you will find. Use it for the day three shakeout and every recovery run after.

Pilates and reformer
How to find it

Reformer studios, Cusco

Small scene · Instagram led

Reformer pilates exists in Cusco but the studios are small, they open and close, and most have no website. Search Instagram for #pilatescusco and #reformercusco and message the active accounts directly. Ask ¿Tienen clases sueltas para visitantes? and expect a reply within a few hours. Drop in rates run around S/ 50 to 70.

Do this in your first two days while you are not training anyway. It takes a day to sort out.

Reliable alternative

Yoga studios in San Blas

San Blas and centre

The yoga scene is much more established than pilates here. Healing House and Yoga Sanasana both run regular drop in classes in English and Spanish, and several San Blas studios do the same. Restorative and yin classes are the right choice in the acclimatization window when nothing strenuous is allowed.

Pole
Realistic view

Pole in Cusco

Very small scene

Be prepared for this one to come up empty. Cusco is small and pole here is a handful of instructors rather than studios. Search Instagram for #polecusco and #poledanceperu, and note that the Peruvian pole community is centred on Lima and Arequipa. If nothing surfaces in the first few days, the aerial and silks classes attached to the San Blas yoga studios are the closest substitute for the same overhead strength work.

Worth asking directly at Healing House. The movement community here is small and everyone knows everyone.

06 · The Haul

Alpaca, vicuña, and San Blas workshops

Cusco is one of the best shopping cities in South America for one specific reason: this is where the world's finest animal fibre comes from. Understanding the difference between what is sold to tourists and what is actually good is the whole game.

The fibre ladder, cheapest to finest: acrylic blend, alpaca, baby alpaca, royal alpaca, vicuña. If a "baby alpaca" scarf costs S/ 30 in a market it is acrylic. Real baby alpaca starts around S/ 250. Vicuña is the finest natural fibre in the world and a scarf runs into four figures in dollars.
The best

Kuna by Alpaca 111

Plaza Regocijo and Plaza de Armas

The premium end of Peruvian fibre, properly graded and honestly labelled. Baby alpaca, royal alpaca, and a vicuña range. Modern cuts rather than tourist knitwear. Prices are fixed and fair and the quality genuinely justifies them. Start here to calibrate your eye before you look anywhere else.

Premium

Sol Alpaca

Plaza Nazarenas and Santa Teresa

The Michell group's retail arm, and the main competitor to Kuna at the top end. Excellent coats and throws, a strong vicuña selection, and a beautiful shop at Plaza Nazarenas. Slightly more classic styling than Kuna.

Design

Hilo

Carmen Alto 260, San Blas

A small designer studio making limited run and bespoke pieces from Peruvian fibre. Contemporary, sculptural, and nothing like anything else in the city. If you want one piece that nobody else will have, this is where it comes from.

They will make to measure if you commission early in the stay.

Silver

Ilaria

Centre and hotel boutiques

Peru's high end silver house. Serious jewellery and objects, properly made, with the pricing that implies. The reliable answer for a gift that needs to look like one.

Workshops

San Blas artisan quarter

Cuesta San Blas and Hatunrumiyoc

The historic craft barrio, and the workshops here are the real thing rather than resellers. The Mendívil workshop is known worldwide for its elongated neck saints, and the Mérida family have been making expressive ceramic figures for generations. Both sell from the studios where the work is made. Walk the hill slowly and go into everything.

Steep cobbled climb. Save it until you are acclimatized.

Textiles

Centro de Textiles Tradicionales del Cusco

Av. El Sol 603

A nonprofit that works with weaving communities across the region to keep traditional techniques alive. Weavers demonstrate on site, every piece is documented with the community and the weaver who made it, and the money goes back. The most meaningful textile purchase available in Cusco and the quality is exceptional.

Volume

Centro Artesanal Cusco

Av. El Sol, south end

Hundreds of stalls under one roof at the bottom of El Sol. This is where you buy the gifts, the blankets, the hats, and the things you are happy to haggle over. Mostly blends rather than pure fibre, and priced accordingly. Bargaining is expected and should be friendly. Start at about sixty percent.

Mall

Real Plaza Cusco

Av. Collasuyo

The modern mall. International chains, a cinema, a food court, Plaza Vea supermarket, and Bodytech. Not why you came, but the practical answer for anything you forgot to pack, including cold weather layers.

07 · The Pantry

Groceries and stocking the kitchen

Cusco has no Whole Foods equivalent, but it has something arguably better: a supermarket that carries the imported and premium range, and a produce market economy that is cheaper and fresher than anything at home. Use both.

Superfoods are local staples here, not health shop items. Quinoa, maca, chia, lucuma, cacao, and purple corn are all sold by weight in the markets for a fraction of the price you have ever paid. Buy them here.
High end pick

Orion Supermarket

Av. El Sol, central

The premium supermarket in Cusco and the one to make your default. Orion carries the imported goods, the better wine and spirits, decent cheese and deli, quality produce, and the international brands the other chains do not stock. Central, walkable from most of the historic centre, and open late. If you only use one supermarket for the twelve days, use this one.

Best stocked for imported breakfast items, nut butters, and anything you would miss from home.

Largest

Plaza Vea

Real Plaza, Av. Collasuyo

The big national chain, inside the mall. Widest overall range and the best prices on bulk items like the large water jugs you will get through at this altitude. Less refined than Orion but it has everything. Pair it with a Bodytech session so you only make the trip once.

Convenience

Mega and La Canasta

Scattered through the centre

Smaller neighbourhood supermarkets dotted around the historic centre. Good for the 8 PM top up on water, fruit, and breakfast things without a ride. Limited range, entirely adequate for the daily gaps.

Produce

Mercado San Pedro

Thupac Amaru · 15 min walk

The main market and the best value in the city by an enormous margin. Fruit, vegetables, cheese, bread, grains by weight, and the juice stalls. Go before 9 AM for the best of it. Bring small notes, bring a bag, and keep your phone in a zipped pocket. Ask for yapa and they will throw in a little extra.

Buy your quinoa, maca, and cacao here by weight rather than packaged in a supermarket. It is a tenth of the price.

Local market

Mercado de Wanchaq

Wanchaq district

Where Cusqueños actually shop, with far fewer tourists than San Pedro and slightly better prices. Same produce, calmer experience, and a good cooked food section upstairs. Worth the extra ten minutes if San Pedro feels like too much.

Run nutrition

Fuelling at altitude

What to pack, what to buy

Bring your own gels and electrolyte tabs. Specialty sports nutrition barely exists in Cusco and what does exist is expensive and limited. Locally, buy bananas, dates, and coca tea from any market, and Gatorade from any supermarket. You need noticeably more electrolyte here than at sea level because the air is bone dry and you lose fluid through breathing without noticing it.

InkaFarma and MiFarma stock rehydration salts (sales de rehidratación) cheaply. Useful after every long effort.

Sleep high. Run the valley. Race Slovenia in the autumn.
